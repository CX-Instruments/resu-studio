# Rewriting a line

`voice.md` says what a line must not be. This says what a good one looks like. Read it
before drafting any `Suggested:` text.

There are no worked before-and-after pairs in this file, and that is deliberate. The
patterns are named instead, at *The patterns to look for*, and the before and after
gets shown on one of the person's own lines. See *Show it on their lines* below for
why.

Whatever the rewrite is, it is built only from what the original already contained or
from an answer the person gave. Nothing is added. Where the stronger line needs a fact
that is not on the record, the correct output is the question rather than the rewrite.

## What a rewrite is for

**Every change has to earn its place against this advertisement.** Not "this reads
better". Better writing that answers nothing is a CV editor's work, and the person
could have had that anywhere. What they came for is the match.

So the question on every proposed line is two-part, and both halves need an answer:

> **Which ask does this now answer that it did not answer before?**
> **Which fact does it rest on?**

If the first has no answer, the line is a style opinion and does not go in the
proposals. If the second has no answer, it is an invention and does not go anywhere.
This is why every entry in `proposing-changes.md` carries `Answers:` and `Draws on:`
and why neither may be empty.

**Three shapes a useful change takes.** Almost everything worth proposing is one of
these:

1. **It surfaces evidence they already have and the reader would not find.** The
   thing was done, and it is buried in a duty, or in the wrong role, or three pages
   in. This is where most of the value is, and it invents nothing.
2. **It says it in the employer's words**, where their own record supports that
   wording. Their term for a thing the person has done. See `voice.md`.
3. **It makes room**, by cutting what this advertisement does not ask for. A page is
   finite, and a line that answers nothing here is spending space a line that answers
   something needs.

**The advertisement decides the order, not just the words.** What this employer leads
with should be near the top of the page. A perfect bullet on page three, answering
their first criterion, is a bullet the reader never reaches.

**And a rewrite that moves nothing is worth saying out loud.** If their line already
answers the ask and simply reads awkwardly, say so and leave it. Rescoring after the
decisions is what proves the work was worth their evening; changes that move the score
nowhere make that number a lie.

## Shorten by cutting repetition, never by cutting content

Everything else in this file tells you what to take out. This section is the floor,
and it matters more than any of them, because a rewrite that loses information has not
been tightened. It has been amputated, and the person cannot see what went.

**Before proposing a shorter line, name what the original said.** Every distinct piece
of information in it: the work, the scale, the constraint it ran under, the standard it
was held to, who it was for, what it was worth. Then check the replacement carries all
of them. A word may go. A piece of information may only go if the same page says it
somewhere else, and then the cut is a duplication cut and says so.

**These are content, not padding, and they survive the cut:**

- **the conditions the work ran under**: a compliance regime, a fixed window, a
  regulated environment, volume, a legacy system, a deadline someone else set
- **the standard it was held to**: auditability, legal accuracy, reconciliation to
  the cent, a regulator's rule
- **who it was for**: the executive, a regulator, a vendor, the public
- **the scale**: how many, how much, how long, across how many systems
- **what it was part of**: the programme, the upgrade, the migration

Strip these and every job in every industry reduces to the same four verbs. They are
the difference between a hard job and an easy one with identical duties, and a reader
who has done the work reads for them.

**A halved line is a warning, not an achievement.** If the replacement is under about
half the length of the original, stop and find what went. Sometimes the answer is that
the original was genuinely half padding, and then the cut is right. Often the answer is
that a clause carrying a constraint went with it.

## Which adjectives survive

The rule is not "cut adjectives". It is **who the adjective is about**.

**An adjective about the person needs evidence.** High-performing, proven,
exceptional, trusted, highly skilled, seasoned. These are the person marking their own
work, a reader discounts them on sight, and they are what the trait rules exist to
remove. If somebody else said it, in an award, a rating or a citation, it is a fact and
it stays, attributed.

**An adjective about the work does not.** Fast-paced, compliance-driven, high-volume,
tightly constrained, regulated, legacy, cross-jurisdictional. These describe the
conditions, not the person's excellence in them. They are the person's own account of
where they worked and they are not a claim about themselves at all. Keep them.

The same sentence often carries one of each. "Led a high-performing team through a
tightly constrained migration window" loses three words and keeps everything that
matters. Cutting both halves is the mistake this section exists to stop.

## Keep their voice, not yours

**A rewrite is their sentence made stronger. It is not a new sentence.**

- **Keep the person.** If the CV is written in the first person, the rewrite is in the
  first person. Verbless third person, "Writes", "Defines", "Manages", is the
  grammar of a position description, and a page written in it reads as though the
  advertisement was pasted back at the employer.
- **Keep the tense.** A current role stays present. Putting a role they still hold
  into the past tense reads as though they have left, and that is a factual error, not
  a style one.
- **Keep their capitalisation of names.** A role title they capitalised is how it was
  called there. Lowercasing it turns a job title into a common noun.
- **Keep the words they chose**, wherever those words are accurate. The point is to
  make their line land, not to replace it with a line you would have written.

Changing any of these is a change like any other, and it needs a reason in `Why:`. If
there is no reason, it is not a change, it is a habit.

## Neutral is not the target

Removing what cannot be verified is not the same as removing all confidence. The
finished page is still a professional document written by somebody who is good at
their job, and it should read that way: assured, specific, and in full sentences that
someone chose. A line can be certain without being inflated, because certainty comes
from the detail being exact rather than from the adjectives.

The failure mode this file has to guard against runs in both directions. On one side,
a line claiming more than the record holds. On the other, a line so stripped it reads
like a timesheet. **The second is not the safe option.** A page of flat duty statements
is a real cost to the person, and "at least it is honest" does not cover it, because
the fuller version was honest too.

## The shape of a bullet

**One claim.** A bullet that says two things is two bullets, or one bullet and a cut.

**Under about thirty words.** A bullet needing a third comma-spliced clause has stopped
being a bullet. Length is not thoroughness; it is the reader's attention being spent
on parsing rather than on the claim.

**Lead with what changed, where something changed. Lead with the work, where nothing
measurably did.** Most duties have no outcome, and inventing one is the failure this
whole skill exists to prevent. A plainly described duty is not a weak bullet.

**Vary the opening.** No more than two bullets in one role should begin with the same
word. Five bullets opening "Led" read as a template and the reader stops seeing the
verb at all.

## The verb

**The strong verb is the one that is true.** Not the unusual one. A verb earns its
place by being specific and accurate, not by being rare. "Ran the forecasting cycle"
is stronger than "orchestrated the forecasting cycle", because a person said the first
one and a thesaurus said the second.

Reach for the word the person would use out loud describing their week. If that word
undersells what they did, that is a question for them, not a licence to upgrade it.

**Words to distrust in your own drafts.** Each of these is almost always a bigger
claim than the record supports, a plain verb wearing a costume, or both:

```
spearheaded   orchestrated   championed   leveraged   utilised
drove         pioneered      transformed  revolutionised
facilitated   engaged with   was instrumental in   played a key role in
```

The last two are the tell. "Played a key role in" is what gets written when the honest
verb would be "contributed to" and that felt too small. It is not too small. It is
what happened, and a reader who has hired people can tell the difference.

## Show it on their lines, never on somebody else's

**Do not illustrate a rule with an invented example.** A worked example has to be
about some kind of work, and whichever kind you choose tells the next person this was
built for somebody else. Somebody whose work looks nothing like the examples concludes
the tool is not for them, and they are reading the very file meant to teach their voice.

This rule governs what is shown to the person: the proposals, the studio, the chat and
anything else they read. The occupational examples inside these reference files are
internal working notes, nobody applying for a job ever sees them, and they stay.

Their CV is the example. It arrived in Phase 1 and it is full of their own weakest
lines, in their own field, about work they can actually talk about. When a rule needs
demonstrating, take one of their lines and show the before and after. That is more
persuasive than any invented pair, because they recognise it.

## The patterns to look for

Named, not illustrated. Each one is a shape you will recognise in any trade.

**The duty restated as a duty.** Opens "Responsible for" or "Duties included". It
describes a job description rather than a person. Ask what they actually did and at
what stakes.

**The scene-setting clause.** Opens with the *reason* for the work rather than the
work. "In order to improve X, undertook a review of Y and implemented improvements" is
two abstract verbs standing where one concrete one goes, and the reader infers the
reason anyway.

This rule is about a stated motive, and it stops there. **A clause describing the
conditions the work ran under is not scene-setting**. It is the hardest information
in the sentence, and cutting it is the single most common way a rewrite ends up
flatter than the line it replaced. See *Shorten by cutting repetition* above.

**The promoted verb.** The draft claims more than the record. It collapses the moment
somebody asks who else was involved. Keep the honest verb for the part they
contributed to, and put the bigger verb only where it is true. Two different verbs in
one line read as more credible than one inflated one.

**The hedge.** "Was instrumental in", "played a key role in". Written when the honest
verb felt too small. Say what they did.

**Trait words in the profile.** Unfalsifiable, and a screener can match nothing in
them to an advertisement. The opening line of a CV should say who they are and where
they have worked.

**The line with nothing in it.** "Managed stakeholders." "Handled customer issues."
No rewrite beats the original, because there is nothing to work with. Polishing
produces a line that sounds more confident and says the same nothing. **Ask instead**,
and say why: a reader will assume the smaller reading of a vague line.

**Two claims in one bullet.** "While also" is almost always the seam where a bullet
should have been split. Both claims are usually good and the first is burying the
second.

**The rounded figure.** "Over 400" is weaker than "432" and it is also less true.
Exact numbers read as somebody who counted. See `arithmetic.md`.

**The repeated opening.** Five bullets starting with the same verb read as a template
and the reader stops seeing it. Each line usually has its own accurate verb; they were
flattened because one word felt strongest.

## The check, before any proposal goes out

Read the suggested line aloud. Three failures show up immediately and almost never on
the page:

1. **It does not survive being said.** Anything you would not say to a colleague in a
   corridor is written for nobody.
2. **You cannot say who did it.** If the sentence would be equally true of the whole
   team, it is not their bullet yet.
3. **You would not want the follow-up question.** If "tell me more about that one"
   would be uncomfortable, the line has drifted past the record. Take it back.

And one more, read the other way round, against the line it replaces:

4. **Something in the original is no longer there.** Read the current line, then the
   suggestion, and name every piece of information in the first that is missing from
   the second. If anything on that list is a condition, a standard, a scale or a
   stakeholder, put it back. If the list is long, the rewrite is a cut wearing a
   rewrite's clothes, and it should be proposed as a cut with its own reason.
