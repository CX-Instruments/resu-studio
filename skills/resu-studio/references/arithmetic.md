# Numbers, dates and years

Every rule here exists because getting it wrong is both easy and visible. The dates
are printed on the same page as the claim, and anyone can add them up.

## Career span claims

**A span claim is measured from the start of the first qualifying role to the end of
the last one.** It is not the sum of the qualifying periods with the gaps subtracted.

Worked example. Somebody claims "seven years leading teams". Their roles:

- Leading role A, Jan 2019 to Apr 2022
- A role, Aug 2022 to May 2023, not leading
- A four month career break in between
- Leading role B, May 2023 to Jun 2026

Summing the two leading roles gives six years four months, and concluding "six years,
not seven" is wrong. Nobody states a career span as a sum of qualifying time net of
gaps, and no reader reads it that way. Jan 2019 to Jun 2026 is seven years five
months, so seven years is accurate and rounds down.

**What to check instead:**

- Does the span start at a role that genuinely qualifies for the claim being made?
- Does it end at one?
- Is the stated figure at or below the elapsed span? Rounding down is safe. Rounding
  up past the next integer is not.
- What sits inside the span that does not qualify? That is not a fault, it is the
  question the person should be ready for if a panel walks the dates.

## Never round up

Seven years five months states as seven. Six years eleven months states as six, or as
"nearly seven", not as seven. The person has to defend the number in a room.

## Figures attached to lists

If a year count and a list of sectors, tools or clients sit in the same sentence, the
reader attaches them to each other. Every item in the list must fall inside the
window.

"7+ years delivering X across three sectors and
financial services" fails if the financial services roles ended before the seven year
window began. Fix by separating: "7+ years delivering X across three sectors
services and telecommunications, following earlier roles in financial services". Both
facts survive and neither needs defending.

## Repeated figures

If the same number appears for two different teams, projects or outcomes, flag it. It
may be true. It also reads as a transcription slip, and a person cannot tell which a
panel will assume. Ask before it prints.

## No figure, no problem

If the person confirms no figure exists for an outcome, the line prints without one
and without an intensifier standing in for one. "Cut reporting turnaround and widened
data access" is a result. "Dramatically reduced reporting turnaround" is the same
sentence with a word that invites the question the person cannot answer.

## Estimates

If the person gives an estimate rather than a counted figure, print it as an estimate.
"Let's say 30 percent" becomes "around a third", not "30%". The hedge is the honest
form and it costs nothing.

## Tense follows dates

A role with an end date is written in past tense throughout: the role paragraph, every
bullet, and any mention in the profile. A single "currently" or a present tense verb
left behind in a role that has ended is a factual error, not a style slip. When a date
line changes, sweep the whole role.
